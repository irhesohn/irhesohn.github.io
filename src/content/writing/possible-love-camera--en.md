Lee Chang-dong's new film, *Possible Love* (가능한 사랑), reportedly began when he took on one installment of a project reimagining Krzysztof Kieślowski's television series *Dekalog* for the present. The commandment assigned to Lee, which also appears as the film's epigraph in its opening shot, was "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbor's goods." Yet what came to mind after I watched the film was not *Dekalog* but another Kieślowski film, *Camera Buff* (1979).

There was a time, no longer, when I was fond of psychoanalysis and Žižek, and during that period I read a book called *The Fright of Real Tears*. "The fright of real tears" is Kieślowski's own phrase. While working on a documentary, he watched a young father cry and came face to face with the enormous ethical responsibility the documentary form carries: Do I have the right to film this man's real tears? In fiction film you can produce tears with glycerin, but the tears inside a documentary frame are real. Žižek points out that the desire to see them, or to record them, is no different from a pornographic desire. Honestly, I no longer remember much of what Žižek did with Kieślowski. What stayed with me is that he named *Camera Buff* as the key work of the moment when the director, prompted by "the fright of real tears," began turning toward fiction, and I wanted to see that film for a long time. I only found it, on DVD in a university library, after I came to the United States for graduate school.

Here is the plot as best I remember it. A factory worker in communist Poland buys an 8mm amateur camera to film his soon-to-be-born baby. Hearing about it, his boss suggests he use the camera to film a company event and make a promotional film. With film stock supplied by the company, he happily shoots the event and edits it into a short piece, which catches the eye of the amateur film world and even wins a prize at an amateur film festival in Warsaw. He falls deep into the world of cinephilia. Eventually one of his films is broadcast on television, and that film, about a worker with dwarfism, inadvertently exposes his boss's money problem (I can't quite remember whether it was embezzled funding or misappropriation). As a result, a plan to build a kindergarten collapses and his mentor at the factory loses his job. When orders come down from above to cut the offending part, he does not re-edit the film according to the censors; he exposes the negative to light and destroys it.

Meanwhile his wife grows sick of his obsession with capturing the world on camera. They had splurged on the camera to record their family and their child growing up, yet her husband claims, of all things, to have found "the meaning of life" in it. She threw a plate at him... I think? Or not? In any case, she hated his love of film. She even tells him, as he heads to Warsaw with his film, not to come back with a prize, and in the end she leaves with the child. In the final scene he turns the camera on himself. I can still see the close-up of his face, flinching as the film starts to roll.

Factory workers, documentary, the people who stand before the camera, and above all the unintended consequences that follow. There is no way to know whether *Possible Love* had *Camera Buff* in mind, but the two films overlap quite a bit. Both ask about the power of the person holding the camera and the inscrutable ethical distance that comes with it. In *Possible Love*, however, the ethical problem arises from the class difference between the person behind the camera and the person in front of it. *Camera Buff*, by putting the camera in a worker's hands, locates ethics in the power that anyone who possesses a medium acquires, regardless of intention, good or bad, and in the consequences of that power. It places at the center not class itself but media as a condition through which class is made. This difference probably has something to do with the difference between communist Poland in the late 1970s and financial-capitalist South Korea in the twenty-first century. Well, a proper comparison will have to wait until I see *Camera Buff* again.

Still, it is worth noting that *Possible Love*, for all that it makes a documentary filmmaker its protagonist, has rather little to say about the narrative conditions that the medium itself creates. The documentary filmmaker Yeji's encounters with Miok and Hoseok would be impossible without the camera, that is, without the occasion of making a documentary. It is through the camera that Yeji can enter the world of laid-off workers. Yeji is the one who possesses the medium. She has a crew she works with, she always carries a camera and tripod, and for the interview at the country house she deliberately sets up a large round light behind the camera. Hoseok shows his face in front of the soft light, while Yeji, interviewer and director, sits with her back to it. Because of the backlight, Yeji's face and the camera beside her are a little darker than Hoseok's face across from them. When Hoseok finally opens up and tells his story, he is looking at Yeji and the camera in the shadows, and at the light shining brighter than both.

This setup lays bare the violence inherent in audiovisual recording. Knowing this violence from experience, documentary filmmakers take great care in bringing interviewees to that seat and drawing difficult stories out of them. That care rests on mutual trust. Yeji, with her "good intentions," has no such concern. But neither does *Possible Love*.

About halfway through, I felt that the film was trying, more than I had expected, to confront the trauma of the laid-off workers. When Hoseok attends group counseling, the film takes considerable time to convey the participants' stories. Soon, however, it overwrites those stories with the story of Sangwoo and Yeji, and so becomes a film about Yeji's hypocrisy. For a director making a film about a director who films laid-off workers, that hypocrisy could hardly have been someone else's problem. So this is also a film about the ethical anguish of hypocrisy, which must have felt painfully close to the director himself. In that sense, the sex scene between Sangwoo and Yeji near the end, which seemed so unnecessary, may have been exactly the right choice. It simply locks Yeji into the position of the hypocrite. Like Yeji, anchored in that sex and the aimless conversation that follows, the film leaves the creator's ethical struggle with hypocrisy stranded there too. The struggle is enormous, but there is neither a thread toward resolution nor the will to find one.

It is not that the film never shows Yeji's ugliness as the one holding the camera. But that ugliness is recuperated as the ugliness of Yeji as an individual who harbors the desire to record, not as the ugliness of the camera or of the desire to record itself. As *Camera Buff* shows, and as the long-standing concerns of countless independent documentary filmmakers attest, the hierarchy between subject and object arises from the very presence of the camera. Filmmakers have tried in many ways to work through this ethical problem with the camera. Some stepped in front of the camera and exposed themselves to the same visual violence; some placed the violence of the camera itself before the camera; and some, like Kieślowski, chose a fictional world instead of capturing physical reality.

*Possible Love*, however, reduces the violence of the camera to the violence of the "person" holding it. Whether it is Yeji or Sangwoo, emotionless behind a mask of kindness, the violence of the camera very quickly becomes one with the violence of capital. What gets emphasized, then, is not the story of the person who exposed themselves to the camera in spite of the violence, but the hypocrisy of the person wielding it. The "good will" of someone who wants, at all costs, to tell their story, knowing it is hypocrisy, knowing it is violence, and knowing that their story may someday become an object of criticism, recedes into the background.

So the greatest hypocrisy, as I see it, is not that people who dare to have everything condescendingly pitied the lives of laid-off workers and tried to capture their stories. The greater hypocrisy is to expose this while never saying what one intends to do, why, or how, and simply pointing at the problem. Who doesn't know the impossibility of solidarity, in theory and in practice? The question is the will to do it anyway. The good will of the person who decides to stand in front of the camera, and the good will of the person who decides to pick up the camera even if accused of hypocrisy. The place where the two meet begins to appear only when one thinks desperately hard about the camera as a condition. That is what I want to see.
