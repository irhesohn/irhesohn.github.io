In the summer of 2023, I had the honor of being among the 240 voters for the Korean Film Archive's 2024 list of [the 100 Best Korean Films](https://www.kmdb.or.kr/eng/list/242). The Archive asked each voter to select ten films from 1934 to 2022 that they believed would be significant in Korean film history.

The decision was difficult to make. I had not seen all of the films released between those years (who would have?), and I was fully aware that the practice of compiling a list, particularly by a state-affiliated institution, contributes to the canonization of specific films in the context of Korean film history. It's like saying, "Ta-da, here are the films you should watch if you take Korean cinema seriously!" Or, this puts pressure on film scholars: "Go watch those 100 films before you say you know about Korean cinema!" So, as a Korean film expert, I felt as if I were compiling my own canon. Am I in any position to do so?

In certain ways, a list like this replicates itself. For example, *Citizen Kane* topped the *Sight and Sound* poll for decades before *Vertigo* replaced it in 2012; [*Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles* in turn displaced *Vertigo* in 2022](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/greatest-film-all-time-jeanne-dielman-23-quai-du-commerce-1080-bruxelles). Aside from the significance of a female filmmaker placing her film at number one for the first time, *Citizen Kane*'s long reign exemplifies the circular logic of this practice. The higher a film is ranked, the more likely viewers are to see it. And the more people who view it, the better its chances of making the cut and even ranking higher. Perhaps *Jeanne Dielman* will be on that list ten years later because now more people have reason to watch it.

While the poll results repeat themselves, it's intriguing to learn how individual voters arrived at such a result. I'm not patient enough to go through [each list](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/greatest-films-all-time/all-voters), but I'm especially interested in Asian voters' choices for national cinema. In their lists, some people make genuinely striking decisions. For example, only two of fifteen Japanese voters chose *Tokyo Story*, which [ranked fourth in the *Sight and Sound* poll](https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/65edaa59-6546-57b5-9fa3-9409f93d1383/tokyo-story). How about Korean voters? Only four out of eleven Korean voters included one or more Korean films on their lists, but *Parasite*, a film that no Korean voter chose, tied for 90th.

Of course, access to more films must play a role here. Many of those who chose *Parasite* or *Tokyo Story* may be generally unfamiliar with Korean or Japanese cinema. And as a Korean, a voter may have felt compelled to introduce a new name for worldwide cinephiles to learn about. Or, I suspect, they may have simply wished to set themselves apart from the Western film critics who know little about Korean cinema but audaciously brag about their minuscule knowledge.

Coming back to the Korean Film Archive vote, I had similar trouble choosing my ten. A few titles came to mind immediately as the ones sure to top the list. Should I follow that consensus? For some of them, I agreed. But what about the others? There were also lesser-known films that I think are as good as, if not better than, those frontrunners. How could I simply let *Parasite* win? And how much weight should I give the established canon of Korean cinema history? Say, *The Housemaid*? Should I add it?

So, what did I choose? Before I present my own list here, let's see the 2024 result. For comparison, the top ten in 2024 and 2014 are shown below.

## Reading the Results

Not surprising to see *The Housemaid* at the top in 2024 again. But this time, it wasn't tied with anything else, as *Aimless Bullet* and *The March of Fools* went down. Notably, the slots between those three films were filled by more recent productions such as *Memories of Murder*, *Parasite*, and *Oldboy*. In fact, with the exception of those three "classic" films, all the other films in the top ten date from the mid-1990s or later, pushing the past lower down. How did this occur?

Yun Seo-yeon and Chung Chong-hwa from the Korean Film Archive, who managed the poll, [discuss the changes they have made to the poll itself](https://www.koreafilm.or.kr/kofa/news/webzine/BC_0000061187) (in Korean). The poll request I received, for example, included a general guideline but also encouraged voters to select works that call for rediscovery and reassessment even if they drew little notice from critics or the public at the time, as well as works that reflect social issues such as human-rights sensitivity and minority issues. This surely prompted many people, including myself, to think outside the box of canonized Korean films and include other notable films on a variety of topics.

This, however, does not entirely explain how recent films have replaced all-time classics like *Madame Freedom*. (Personally, I was partly relieved to see those 1970s and 1980s films slide out of the top ten.) According to Yun and Chung in the linked interview, the number of voters nearly quadrupled in 2024, and the voter pool now includes not only people in the industry and academia but also those in adjacent fields of arts and culture such as poets, novelists, and artists. This resulted in recent films outranking older pictures. Access to older films is still significantly limited compared to newer ones. The more familiar you are with a film, the more likely you are to consider it emblematic of Korean cinema. The 2024 list of Korean cinema's canon reveals more about the present than about Korean cinema or its history. This moment of canonization, in other words, shows how contemporary voters perceive Korean cinema.

This list reveals three tendencies in the voters' perception of Korean cinema. First, according to this list, Korean cinema has become synonymous with two filmmakers: Bong Joon-ho and Park Chan-wook. Each has two films on the list, one of which was their latest film at the time of the vote. This identification of Korean cinema with two auteurs is more than a simple association: it also aligns the voters' perspective on Korean cinema with that of global film culture. At the very least, the voters want Park and Bong to represent Korean cinema, as they are the two filmmakers best known and appreciated around the world. The ["inward-looking" approach](https://academic.oup.com/screen/article-abstract/30/4/36/1614862), proposed by Andrew Higson decades ago, is no longer working here.

The alignment of home and foreign perspectives demonstrates how national cinema is conceptualized in the dynamics (and conflicts) between domestic and international audiences. Korean cinema is not defined by its industrial practices, which include production, distribution, exhibition, and consumption. Because those practices have spread globally and virally, each sector of the industry no longer exists purely within geographical boundaries. Korean cinema, identified with Bong's and Park's directing careers, is partly the result of internalizing such outside views.

I am not saying that the voters are passive in the face of global pressure. Instead, they take part in the dynamic redefinition of national cinema through global circulation, and in doing so they also refashion their position in the world, which is inextricably linked to the expansion of their physical and mental horizon of experience. In the case of global Korean cinema, I think the voters identify with the global audience, placing themselves outside of Korea.

Secondly, the rise of films from the late 1990s and early 2000s resonates with [the recent discussion of the era as the "origin" of Korean cinema's current worldwide stature](https://www.ln.edu.hk/daci/koreancinema2023). This "renaissance" period was not only about the new-generation filmmakers who freed themselves from the past mode of film production, culminating in the two filmmakers mentioned above. It was contingent upon the emergence of new-generation audiences from all kinds of cultural backgrounds: the consumption culture embodied by Generation X or the "orange tribe" (*orenji-jok*, affluent young Seoulites of the early 1990s known for flaunting their consumption), a new sensibility about freedom of speech and self-expression after the formal democratization, and, of course, the belated boom in cinephile (or videophile) groups keen on film aesthetics. Many of the seeds for today's Korean popular culture are considered to have been sown in that era; its globalization is thus the harvest of the past. Bong's and Park's names do not only reflect their own cinematic achievement; the 1990s and the early 2000s are also clearly etched as the source of their success in the narrative of the past. By making an origin story, the list above confirms such an official and conventional approach to the historiography of Korean cinema.

Lastly, I cannot look away from the presence of three "classic" films that still made the top ten: *The Housemaid*, *Aimless Bullet*, and *The March of Fools*. Their standing as the canon of Korean cinema has essentially become fixed with the 2024 list. The first tendency I mentioned above shows up again here: *The Housemaid* was preferred over the other two, both of which had tied with it in 2014. Thanks to Martin Scorsese's [World Cinema Project](https://www.film-foundation.org) and to [Bong's short commentary on the film](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rh5QzhNNi3k), it is relatively better known to viewers worldwide. Here are some questions I want to investigate further: What can the three films tell us about how Korean cinema is being defined in South Korea? What do they stand for? Why *Aimless Bullet*, but not *Madame Freedom*? Or why *The March of Fools*, but not *My Mother and Her Guest*? I may return to these questions later.

## My Ten Picks

All right! To wrap up this first (and long) post, [here are my picks](https://www.kmdb.or.kr/db/list/242/0003/voters/detail/368) (the linked page is in Korean), listed in the order of the release date. Though I can't promise a regular series, I will try to write something about each of them, sometimes very brief, sometimes extremely long. I added a "Watch" link under the title if the film is available for online viewing. I hope you'll watch the films before I write about them!

### *Spring of the Korean Peninsula* **반도의 봄** (Yi Pyŏng-il 이병일, 1941)

▶ [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T0MiZnCvb7s)

> A romance of colonized filmmakers, painted by two cameras facing each other.

### *Madame Freedom* **자유부인** (Han Hyung-mo 한형모, 1956)

▶ [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FkAbVQhfpmw)

> What the men of the time feared was not the licentiousness of the "Madame Freedoms," but the challenge of women who had begun to achieve economic independence.

### *The Bell Tower* **종각(또 하나의 새벽을 그리며)** (Yang Chu-nam 양주남, 1958)

▶ [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cxpUjKQqvKA)

> The memory of the Japanese military "comfort women" was already vividly alive in 1958.

### *The Housemaid* **하녀** (Kim Ki-young 김기영, 1960)

> A masterpiece that proves controversial every time you watch it again. It shows the limits of its time and its transcendence of that time at once.

### *The Girl Like the Sun* **태양닮은 소녀** (Lee Man-hee 이만희, 1974)

> An exploding camera. Lee Man-hee's answer to the 1970s, an era in which youthful freedom and institutional repression were intermingled.

### *Gilsotteum* **길소뜸** (Im Kwon-taek 임권택, 1985)

▶ [Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UtDHc881l34)

> Im Kwon-taek depicts *Finding Dispersed Families*, the biggest media event ever, with such a cold, dispassionate gaze.

### *Chilsu and Mansu* **칠수와 만수** (Park Kwang-su 박광수, 1988)

> The camera, wandering between *minjung* (the "people") and the intelligentsia, asks itself how far it stands from the helicopter's power-laden gaze.

### *Marriage Story* **결혼이야기** (Kim Ŭi-sŏk 김의석, 1992)

> The beginning of the "planned film." The success of commercial film. If this film still looks progressive, Korean society has regressed that much.

### *Take Care of My Cat* **고양이를 부탁해** (Jeong Jae-eun 정재은, 2001)

> It feels new whenever you watch it. It transcends its era as a masterpiece of women's cinema and youth cinema, yet it is also highly valuable as a faithful record of the 2000s.

### *The World of Us* **우리들** (Yoon Ga-eun 윤가은, 2016)

> It observes children's society through an ethnographic gaze. The beginning of Yoon Ga-eun's world, and the beginning of a new world of Korean cinema.
